# Light Intensity and the Rate of Photosynthesis: A Floating Leaf Disk Assay

## Abstract

This investigation used the floating leaf disk assay to test whether light intensity affects the rate of net photosynthesis in leaf disks. Ten vacuum-infiltrated disks were submerged in sodium bicarbonate solution under two conditions: a Part 1 baseline using standard indoor classroom light, and a Part 2 treatment using bright outdoor sunlight. Photosynthetic rate was measured indirectly using ET₅₀, the time for half the disks to regain buoyancy, expressed as a rate via 1/ET₅₀. It was hypothesized that greater light intensity would produce a shorter ET₅₀, since light drives the light-dependent reactions that ultimately release oxygen. The baseline ET₅₀ was 39.7 minutes (1/ET₅₀ ≈ 0.025 disks/min), while the outdoor-light treatment reached its ET₅₀ in 16.0 minutes (1/ET₅₀ ≈ 0.063 disks/min), a roughly 148% increase in rate. The data supported the hypothesis: higher light intensity substantially increased the net rate of photosynthesis.

## Introduction

Photosynthesis converts light energy into chemical energy stored in sugar, releasing oxygen as a byproduct:

{{< katex displayMode=true >}}
6CO_2 + 6H_2O \xrightarrow{\text{light energy}} C_6H_{12}O_6 + 6O_2
{{< /katex >}}

A leaf's spongy mesophyll normally holds air in its intercellular spaces, which is why an untreated leaf disk floats. Pulling that air out with a vacuum causes the disk to sink. Submerging the sunken disk in a sodium bicarbonate solution supplies the leaf with an abundant alternate source of CO₂. As photosynthesis proceeds, the oxygen it produces accumulates in those same air spaces, and the disk gradually regains buoyancy and floats again.

The leaf is simultaneously respiring, which consumes oxygen, so the rate at which disks float is really a measure of *net* photosynthesis, gross photosynthesis minus respiration, not photosynthesis in isolation. Because a single disk can float unevenly or become stuck, the class used ET₅₀ ("effective time 50"), the time at which 50% of disks (5 of 10) are floating, as a standard reference point. The median is used instead of the mean because an occasional disk that never rises would distort an average. Since ET₅₀ measures *time*, a longer ET₅₀ actually means *slower* photosynthesis; to express this as a rate that increases when photosynthesis speeds up, 1/ET₅₀ is calculated instead.

This raises the experimental question explored in Part 2 of this investigation: what factors affect the rate of photosynthesis, and specifically, does increasing light intensity increase the rate of net photosynthesis in leaf disks?

## Hypothesis (Part 2)

If the light intensity increases, then the ET₅₀ will decrease and the rate of photosynthesis (1/ET₅₀) will increase, because light is a reactant in the light-dependent reactions, more light energy drives greater production of ATP and NADPH, which in turn fuels a faster Calvin cycle and faster oxygen release into the leaf's air spaces.

## Materials and Methods

The Part 1 baseline used a sodium bicarbonate solution prepared at 10 g of baking soda per 500 mL of water, combined with a dilute soap solution of 22 drops of liquid soap per 500 mL of water. Ten leaf disks were punched from fresh leaves using a hole punch, avoiding major veins. One drop of the dilute soap solution was added to the bicarbonate solution without generating suds. The ten disks were placed in a 10 mL plastic syringe barrel along with a small volume of the bicarbonate/soap solution; the plunger was replaced and most of the air was pushed out without crushing the leaves. The tip was sealed with a finger and the plunger was pulled back to create a vacuum, held for approximately 10 seconds, then released so the solution infiltrated the disks. This was repeated as needed until the disks sank. Once sunk, the disks were transferred into a cup of fresh bicarbonate solution and placed under a standard indoor light source. A second set of disks was prepared identically and placed in a control cup of deionized water only. Every minute, the number of floating disks in each cup was recorded, with gentle swirling to dislodge any disks stuck to the container, continuing until all disks floated or a maximum of 20 minutes elapsed.

For Part 2, the identical procedure was repeated with one deliberate change: instead of standard indoor classroom lighting, the prepared disks were placed under intense outdoor sunlight. Disk size, leaf size, solution volume, bicarbonate concentration, and soap concentration were all held constant between the baseline and treatment trials so that light intensity was the only variable being tested. As in Part 1, the number of floating disks was recorded every minute for up to 20 minutes.

## Discussion

Photosynthesis is an essential process for life because it is the ultimate energy source for nearly every ecosystem: producers use it to build the chemical energy that supports every other organism in a food chain. This lab provided direct evidence of that process occurring, since oxygen produced during photosynthesis refilled the mesophyll air spaces of the vacuum-treated leaf disks, causing them to become buoyant again after the air was originally extracted.

ET₅₀ and the rate of photosynthesis are inversely related: as the rate of photosynthesis increases, ET₅₀ decreases, because a faster reaction needs less time for 5 of the 10 disks to float. Because a smaller number intuitively reading as "slower" is confusing on a rate axis, 1/ET₅₀ is calculated instead, it produces a value that increases when photosynthesis speeds up, making it a more direct and readable expression of rate than ET₅₀ itself.

Comparing the two conditions quantitatively (Table 1, Table 2, and the ET₅₀ Summary table): the Part 1 baseline ET₅₀ was extrapolated to 39.7 minutes (1/ET₅₀ ≈ 0.025 disks/min), since only 3 of 10 disks had floated by the 20-minute cutoff. The Part 2 outdoor-light treatment reached its ET₅₀ at 16.0 minutes (1/ET₅₀ ≈ 0.063 disks/min), a percent change of about +148% in rate. Figure 2 shows the treatment curve rising well above the baseline curve for nearly the entire trial, Figure 3 shows the treatment crossing the 5-disk line roughly 24 minutes before the baseline would be projected to, and Figure 4 shows the same result expressed as rate, with the treatment bar about 2.5 times taller than the baseline bar. This data supports the hypothesis. Light is a reactant in the light-dependent reactions; more intense light means more energy captured, more ATP and NADPH produced, and a faster Calvin cycle, so more oxygen is released into the mesophyll air spaces in the same amount of time and the disks become buoyant sooner. Because the baseline value is extrapolated rather than directly observed, the exact percent change is an estimate, but even a more conservative baseline estimate would still leave the treatment clearly faster.

One specific source of error was that the bicarbonate solution was not identical between the two trials: the treatment solution appeared less bubbly, suggesting a lower bicarbonate concentration and therefore less available CO₂. Less CO₂ would limit the Calvin cycle and slow oxygen production, which would make the treatment's measured ET₅₀ longer than it should have been, meaning the true effect of light intensity may be even larger than what was measured. A related confounding variable is that the outdoor trial was likely warmer than the indoor trial; temperature affects enzyme activity, so warmer conditions could have contributed to the faster rate independent of light intensity itself.

Zooming out, photosynthesis matters far beyond a single leaf. Globally, it removes CO₂ from the atmosphere and converts it into sugars, making it a central part of the carbon cycle, and it produces the oxygen most organisms depend on for cellular respiration. Because producers form the base of nearly every food web, the energy captured through photosynthesis eventually supports every consumer above them, including humans. These results connect directly to agriculture: since light intensity increased the rate by roughly 148% in this trial, light exposure appears to meaningfully limit how fast plants can photosynthesize. Crop yield depends heavily on how much light plants receive, which is why spacing, shading, and greenhouse lighting design all affect food production, and shifts in light, temperature, or CO₂ availability across an ecosystem could change how much carbon plants absorb and how much energy moves up the food chain.

## Conclusion/Reflection

This investigation showed that light intensity has a large effect on the rate of photosynthesis: because light acts as a reactant in the light-dependent reactions, more intense light drove a substantially faster rate of oxygen production and disk buoyancy. Beyond the specific result, the experience reinforced how to design and control an experiment properly, isolating one variable while holding the rest constant.

The most interesting part of the lab was watching the outdoor-light disks behave almost the opposite of what a slow, steady process might suggest, they stayed essentially still for the first several minutes and then rose rapidly, overtaking and exceeding the total number of disks that had floated indoors in the same amount of time.

The most difficult and frustrating part of the lab was punching the leaf disks, since the hole puncher being used was dull and often left the disks only partially cut out; this was worked through by carefully tearing away the remaining connected tissue by hand.

If this investigation were repeated, the main change would be ensuring the bicarbonate solution was prepared identically for both trials, since the treatment solution appeared noticeably less bubbly than the baseline, suggesting its concentration may have been slightly lower and introducing an uncontrolled variable into the comparison.
